A research journal entry, not investment advice. The model behind it: [`docs/thesis-engine.md`](../thesis-engine.md).

## Why

GPU-hours are becoming a commodity. There are public price pages from a dozen clouds and neoclouds, spot markets, and index products. If compute is a commodity and models are not, the labs should be able to sell tokens at prices that hold up against what the GPUs behind them rent for. If models are a commodity too, token prices should fall toward the cost of compute, demand should spread beyond the labs, and the buildout should slow. This thesis bets on the first reading and names, in advance, what would show the second.

## Method

- **Blended price.** (3 × input + 1 × output) ÷ 4, in USD per million tokens, at each lab's standard-tier list price for its mapped flagship. The flagship is the price matrix's flagship cell: the model the vendor's own page names as the place to start (`layers/terminal/server/utils/terminal-classes.ts`, with the judge's re-mappings laid over it). A lab with no mapped flagship, or a mapped one with no input and output price, is left out and named.
- **GPU price.** The on-demand list price of an NVIDIA H100, in USD per GPU-hour. An instance price is divided by its GPUs. Only SXM/HGX boards count, plus rows that name no form factor (Crusoe, Oracle, Google Cloud). PCIe and NVL cards are stored and not counted, so the median compares the same board across providers. Reserved, spot, preemptible, dedicated-endpoint and multi-week cluster prices are other products and are not read.
- **Median.** Each provider counts once, at the median of its own counted rows (sizes, configurations, regions). The reading is the median of those provider values. Azure's 82 regional SXM rows are one vote, like Crusoe's single row.
- **Quorum.** Claim 1 needs at least 5 H100 providers with a price that day. With fewer, the reading is stored, marked void, and the claim is unresolved; a void reading never feeds the 26-week high.
- **B200.** The same ratio over the B200 median is recorded beside claim 1 and never read by its rule.
- **Spot price (claim 7).** Where a provider's page prints a spot price beside the on-demand price of the same H100 instance, we divide one by the other. A page that prints two spot prices for one instance gives a range, and we take its middle. Each provider counts once, at the median of its rows, and the reading is the median provider. It needs four providers, the same quorum `/compute` holds its combined spot figure to. With fewer, the reading is stored, marked void, and the claim is unresolved.
- **Capex guidance (claim 8).** We read the capex figure each filer's earnings release prints for a fiscal year (`layers/terminal/server/exposure/guidance.ts`). A cut is read on a fiscal year still running: the newest release's midpoint against the highest midpoint an earlier release printed for that year. A landing is read on the year that last ended, once its 10-K is filed: the filed capex against the low end of the last guidance before the filing. A filer counts once, however it falls short.
- **Peak and off-peak (claim 9).** Each lab's flagship gets the same short request once an hour (`/incidents#probes`). For each lab we take the median throughput in its six working hours and in six night hours, per day, then the median of each over the trailing 7 complete days, and divide. A lab is compared only with itself. The reading is the median lab, and it needs four labs with at least three such days each. The share of probes that were rate limited or failed is stored beside it and read by no rule.
- **Sources.** Every number is stored with its source URL and fetch time: list prices in the price tables, GPU prices in `recipe_observations` (one recipe per provider, `layers/terminal/server/theses/recipes/gpu-*.ts`), raw bytes in R2. The audit, terms and exclusions are in [`docs/source-audit.md`](../source-audit.md) and `sources.yaml` (`recipe_sources`, `cut`).

## How the status is set

- Claims 1–3 use a sustained drawdown: each weekly checkpoint (the newest reading, then one 7, 14 and 21 days back) is compared with the high of the window before it. Broken when all four sit 30% or more below; weakening once one does, or once the newest is half way (15%).
- Claim 4 compares the newest count with its 90-day high: broken at a 40% drop, weakening at 20%.
- Claim 5 reads quarters: broken when the two newest are both below 30% growth, weakening when one is.
- Claim 7 uses the same sustained drawdown as claims 1 to 3, against its 90-day high. It needs 111 days of counted readings, and none counts until four providers print a spot price.
- Claim 9 reads a floor at four weekly checkpoints: broken when all four sit below it, weakening once the newest does. It needs no window of history, only the four readings.
- Claim 8 reads the newest count: broken at two filers, weakening at one.
- **History.** A claim is unresolved until its window is full, and its status says how much history it has and how much it needs. Claims 1–3 need the window plus the three weeks their checkpoints reach back: 203 days for claim 1, 201 for claim 2, 111 for claim 3. Claim 4 needs 90 days. Expected first resolutions: claim 4 around 2026-11-23, claim 3 around 2026-12-05, claims 1 and 2 around late May 2027. Claim 5 resolves now.
- **No backfill into the claims.** GPU prices and flagship prices start on the first run, and the class map is code, not a stored history. The Internet Archive does hold copies of most providers' price pages: `scripts/wayback-backfill.ts --axis gpu` reads one a week into `archived_gpu_prices`, and `/compute` charts it as history before our first read. Those rows are event time read back in 2026, so no claim, floor or 26-week high counts them. Claims 3 and 4 are rebuilt from the rankings and job history the terminal already holds (from 2026-08-10 and 2026-08-25).
- **New flagships are ordinary readings.** When a lab's mapped flagship changes, the reading records the change ("OpenAI gpt-6-astra → …") and the thesis page notes it in the readings table, so a step in the series can be explained. The step itself counts.

## Assumptions

- A flagship list price is a fair proxy for what a lab can charge at the frontier. It is not: volume discounts, batch tiers and enterprise contracts are lower, and the mix of tokens sold is not 3:1 everywhere.
- The public on-demand price of an H100 tracks what compute costs. Labs mostly buy on multi-year contracts or own their capacity, so this is the price a marginal renter sees, not the labs' own cost.
- A lab's flagship is served on hardware comparable to an H100. Newer models run on newer GPUs (B200, GB200), which is why the B200 ratio is kept beside it.

## Limits

- **Claim 1 is a price relationship, not a margin.** It assumes no throughput (tokens per GPU-hour) and no serving cost. A falling ratio can mean pricing power is going, or that tokens got cheaper to serve and the lab passed it on. The ratio does not separate the two.
- **List prices, not transaction prices.** At the audit, Vast.ai's marketplace listed H100 SXM offers at a median of $2.40 per GPU-hour, against the first-party median of $4.50. Spot, reserved and negotiated prices sit below the list.
- **The provider set is what passed the audit**, not the market. AWS (its price file is 482 MB, over the fetch cap), Google Cloud (client-rendered page, keyed API) and RunPod (terms bar scrapers) are cut. Azure ($14.38) and Oracle ($10.00) list far above the neoclouds, so the median sits among the neoclouds. A provider dropping out moves it.
- **One page each.** A redesign fails that provider's recipe, which is flagged in the ops digest and shown on `/data`; it is left out of that day's median, never carried forward. Below five H100 providers, claim 1 is unresolved.
- **OpenRouter is one aggregator's traffic, not market share.** Claim 3 sees developer traffic routed through OpenRouter, not direct API or enterprise volume. The daily rankings list the top 50 models and fold the rest into one "other" row, so a lab model outside the top 50 counts as not-a-lab: the share is a lower bound. A kill there prompts a revision, not a verdict.
- **Claim 3 starts near its kill line.** The first reading is 29.5% below the 90-day high of the week to 2026-08-17 and has been lower (42% below at 2026-10-05). The rule needs 111 days of history before it can break, and 2026-08-17 leaves the 90-day window on 2026-11-15.
- **Hiring feeds cover five labs.** OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Mistral (reporting since 2026-11-02) and, from 2026-11-04, Google DeepMind: the DeepMind rows of Google's careers feed, where the `HARDWARE_ENGINEERING` category counts toward the cluster. Meta, DeepSeek, Alibaba and Moonshot AI have no usable public feed, and their roles are not imputed. xAI's board is blended with SpaceX (every row says SpaceXAI) and supplies most of the count, so claim 4 mostly reads xAI's data-center hiring. A lab joining steps the sum up from its first day; a feed that stops reporting steps it down. Either shows in the reading's per-lab breakdown, which is read before a fall is taken as evidence. The cluster's department terms are in `sources.yaml` (`department_clusters`).
- **CoreWeave stands alone.** Nebius files under IFRS (no us-gaap revenue tag to read), IREN's revenue is mostly bitcoin mining, and Applied Digital leases data-center capacity rather than renting GPUs. No comparable is shown. CoreWeave's revenue is concentrated in a few customers, including labs this thesis is about.
- **Claim 6 is Epoch's estimate, and starts unresolved.** 80% of Epoch's power estimates fall within a factor of 1.4; coverage starts with sites operational in 2024 or later, so older fleets are missing. Epoch also revises and adds sites, so the total can step either way on an update. The rule needs 180 days of stored history behind four weekly readings, so the claim cannot break before about 2027-05-29. It was added on 2026-11-04, after the other claims, with the first reading in view.
- **Claim 7 cannot resolve yet.** Three providers print an H100 spot price (CoreWeave, Azure, and Google Cloud from 2026-11-20), and the reading needs four. Until one more does, the claim is unresolved and each provider's figure is shown on its own. Providers set their own spot prices and some rarely change them, so two would not be a market. Lowering the quorum is a revision, to be written here if it is made.
- **Claim 7 reads a fall, not a level.** Spot capacity can be taken back at short notice, so it sells far under on-demand as a matter of course: 40% of on-demand at CoreWeave and 18% at Azure on the captured pages. No fixed share says capacity went slack. The claim breaks when the share falls 30% from its own 90-day high, so it can't break before it has 111 days of counted readings.
- **A spot price is one provider's list price for capacity it can take back.** It isn't a traded price. A provider can hold it steady while its spare capacity fills or empties.
- **Claim 8 reads two filers today.** Meta's releases print a range and Amazon's printed one figure. Alphabet's and Microsoft's releases print none (Microsoft guides on its call, and we don't read transcripts), and none of Oracle's has been read. With two filers the kill needs both. Amazon's filed capex isn't scored, because it guides a net figure and we hold the gross line, so its landing is never read. The claim was registered with these figures in view.
- **A cut in guidance is not a cut in spending.** A filer can lower a figure it set too high and still spend more than the year before. Claim 8 reads the guidance, as its kill condition says.
- **Claim 9 is the weakest claim here, and it is registered anyway.** It had no reading when it was written and nothing shows that probe throughput predicts anything. One account sends one short request an hour through one router, so it misses short bursts. A slow peak can mean a lab is short of capacity, or that demand is strong, and those point opposite ways for this thesis. The 70% line is a judgment, not a measured threshold. A kill here prompts a revision, not a verdict. Review it on 2026-12-19, with a month of readings.
- **Mistral has no flagship.** Its docs name no start-here model (`CLASS_GAPS`), so claims 1 and 2 read five labs.
- **Claims 1 and 2 keep the six labs they opened with.** DeepSeek, Alibaba and Moonshot AI (added 2026-11-04) are left out of the flagship median and named in each reading. Flagship readings are stored as taken and cannot be recomputed, so a median over a set that grew would step on the day a lab joined; Alibaba's $3.00 Qwen 3.8 Max would have taken the median from $8.00 to $6.00, 25% and most of the way to the kill line, with no price having moved.
- **Claim 3 now counts open-weight traffic.** DeepSeek's, Qwen's and Kimi's models are mostly served on OpenRouter by third parties, so the share they add is demand for those labs' models, not traffic the labs serve, as with Meta's Llama line.

## Watched, not counted

These sit beside the claims as context. None sets a status.

- **Who the buildout pays.** `/dependencies#suppliers` lists the chip and compute suppliers whose filings name a lab or a cloud as a customer, each with the passage. `/dependencies#listed` puts every listed company the site ties to a lab in one table. Read them with claim 5: CoreWeave's revenue depends on a few customers, and these tables say which filings name them.
- **Who powers the sites.** `/compute#suppliers` lists the utilities, generators and hosts at the data centers Epoch AI credits to a lab. Read it with claim 6.
- **Deal sizes.** `/ground-truth` scores press reports of a deal's size against the filing that printed it later. A commitment that shrinks between the headline and the filing is a reason to reread claims 5 and 8.
- **Load bounds.** `/compute` shows implied load from free sources, as bounds. It is an estimate with wide error and feeds no claim.
